Parcel Sorting Automation Study    Report OE-2026-14

Automating Parcel Sorting at Regional Hubs
Operations Engineering Group, Fernhill Logistics. November 2026.
Summary.  This  study  compares  four  ways  to  raise  sorting  capacity  at  the  Leeds,  Bristol  and  Glasgow  hubs
ahead of the 2027 peak season. It recommends a phased cross-belt installation at Leeds, a robotic put wall at
Glasgow,  and  continued  manual  sorting  with  handheld  scanners  at  Bristol  until  volumes  justify  a  larger
investment.
1. Introduction
Parcel  volumes  across  the  network  grew  by  6.8
percent in the third quarter of 2026, and the com-
mercial team expects a further 11 percent increase
during  2027.  Each  hub  still  relies  on  manual
sorting  for  most  outbound  parcels,  which  makes
labour  the  largest  single  variable  cost  and  the
main constraint during seasonal peaks.
This  paper  sets  out  the  operational  baseline,  de-
scribes  the  automation  options  that  were  eval-
uated,   and   compares   their   capital   cost   and
payback.  It  closes  with  an  implementation  plan
and a set of recommendations for the 2027 capital
budget.
The  analysis  uses  twelve  months  of  scan  data
from  the  warehouse  management  system,  labour
records  from  the  rostering  platform,  and  quota-
tions  from  three  equipment  suppliers  received  in
October 2026.
2. Current Sorting Operations
Inbound trailers are unloaded onto a single induc-
tion  belt  at  each  hub.  Operators  read  the  desti-
nation label, place the parcel into one of forty roll
cages,  and  close  each  cage  when  it  is  full.  A
typical   operator   sorts   between   420   and   480
parcels  per  hour,  depending  on  parcel  size  and
label quality.
Mis-sorts are the most expensive failure. A parcel
placed  in  the  wrong  cage  travels  to  the  wrong
delivery  depot  and  returns  a  day  later,  which
breaks   the   next-day   promise   and   doubles   the
line-haul  cost.  The  mis-sort  rate  averaged  0.6
percent  across  the  network  in  2026,  rising  to  1.1
percent  in  December  when  temporary  staff  made
up a third of each shift.
Capacity  is  limited  by  floor  space  as  much  as  by
labour.  Leeds  operates  at  94  percent  of  its  cage
positions  during  the  evening  peak,  and  the  site
cannot  add  induction  points  without  moving  the
returns   area.   Bristol   and   Glasgow   have   more
room  but  face  the  same  pattern  of  late-evening
congestion.
Returns add a further load at the end of each shift.
Around 4 percent of outbound parcels come back
through the same induction belt, and they must be
checked,  relabelled  and  sorted  again  before  the
next morning. On busy days this work pushes the
last trailer departure back by up to forty minutes.
Maintenance  demands  are  modest  today  because
the  belts  are  simple  and  spare  parts  are  held  on
site.   Any   automation   option   will   change   that
profile,  so  the  engineering  team  assessed  mainte-
nance requirements alongside capital cost.
3. Automation Options
Four  options  were  evaluated  against  the  same
volume forecast. Each was scored on capital cost,
sorting  rate,  accuracy,  floor  space  and  the  effort
needed to integrate it with the warehouse manage-
ment system.
Option  A  keeps  manual  sorting  but  equips  every
operator  with  a  handheld  scanner  that  confirms
the  correct  cage  before  the  parcel  is  released.
Suppliers  estimate  that  scanner  confirmation  cuts
mis-sorts  by  about  two  thirds  while  leaving  the
sorting rate unchanged.
Option B is a tilt-tray sorter, a proven design that
tips  parcels  into  chutes  at  speeds  of  up  to  9,000
items  per  hour.  It  handles  a  wide  range  of  parcel
sizes but needs a long straight run of floor space,

Option C is a cross-belt sorter. Each carrier has its

Option  D  is  a  robotic  put  wall.  Mobile  robots
carry parcels from the induction point to a grid of
storage  locations,  and  the  layout  can  grow  one
module  at  a  time.  The  sorting  rate  per  module  is
lower,  but  the  modular  design  suits  sites  where
volumes are uncertain.
All  four  options  were  also  reviewed  for  energy
use,  noise  and  fire  safety.  None  of  them  requires
changes   to   the   sprinkler   layout,   although   the
cross-belt    and    tilt-tray    designs    would    need
additional  fire  doors  where  the  loop  crosses  the
existing mezzanine floor.
4. Cost and Payback
Capital  costs  include  equipment,  installation  and
commissioning,  and  the  integration  work  needed
to connect each system to the warehouse manage-
ment  system.  Payback  is  calculated  from  labour
savings  and  avoided  mis-sort  costs  at  forecast
2027 volumes, before financing costs.
Table 1. Capital cost and payback by option
Option    Capital (GBP) Payback (months)
A: Handheld scanners    180,000    14
B: Tilt-tray sorter    1,450,000    31
C: Cross-belt sorter    2,100,000    38
D: Robotic put wall    960,000    22
Option  A  pays  back  fastest  but  does  not  add
capacity.  Options  B  and  C  deliver  the  largest
capacity  gains,  while  option  D  sits  between  the
two  on  both  cost  and  payback.  Sensitivity  tests
show   that   a   ten   percent   shortfall   against   the
volume  forecast  extends  the  payback  of  option  C
by roughly seven months.
Maintenance   contracts   add   between   4   and   7
percent  of  the  capital  cost  each  year  for  the
automated options. These charges are included in
the  payback  figures  above,  together  with  the  cost
of  two  additional  technicians  at  each  automated
site.
If  volumes  exceed  the  forecast,  the  automated
options  pay  back  sooner.  A  volume  increase  of
ten percent above plan would shorten the payback
of option C to about 31 months and of option D to
about 18 months.
5. Implementation Plan
The  proposed  sequence  starts  with  the  quickest
win. Handheld scanners would be rolled out to all
three  hubs  in  the  first  quarter  of  2027,  giving
immediate  accuracy  gains  and  building  the  data
needed to size the larger systems.
A  cross-belt  sorter  would  then  be  installed  at
Leeds  between  April  and  August  2027.  Instal-
lation  would  take  place  in  two  phases  so  that  the
existing  manual  lines  can  keep  running,  with  the
second  phase  timed  to  finish  before  the  autumn
ramp-up.
Glasgow would receive a four-module robotic put
wall  in  the  third  quarter  of  2027,  with  space
reserved  for  two  further  modules.  Bristol  would
continue with scanner-assisted manual sorting and
be  reassessed  when  its  evening  volume  exceeds
1,200   parcels   per   hour   for   four   consecutive
weeks.
Each installation includes a four-week parallel run
during which manual sorting remains available as
a  fallback.  Training  for  operators  and  mainte-
nance  staff  begins  six  weeks  before  each  go-live
date.
A small project team will manage the programme,
led   by   the   head   of   network   engineering   and
including  a  site  lead  from  each  hub,  a  finance
partner  and  an  IT  integration  manager.  The  team
will  hold  a  fortnightly  review  with  each  supplier
during installation.

6. Risks and Mitigations
The  largest  risk  is  a  delay  in  equipment  delivery.
Suppliers  currently  quote  lead  times  of  22  to  30
weeks  for  cross-belt  systems,  so  the  purchase
order  for  Leeds  must  be  placed  by  the  end  of
January  2027  to  protect  the  August  completion
date.
Integration   with   the   warehouse   management
system  is  the  second  risk.  The  current  interface
handles   batch   updates   every   fifteen   minutes,
while   automated   sorters   need   real-time   desti-
nation  lookups.  The  IT  team  has  scheduled  an
interface  upgrade  for  February  2027  and  will  test
it against recorded peak traffic.
Staff    acceptance    matters    as    much    as    the
technology.  Operators  will  move  from  sorting  to
induction, exception handling and quality checks,
and  the  human  resources  team  will  consult  staff
representatives    before    any    changes    to    shift
patterns are confirmed.
Construction  work  inside  a  live  building  is  a
further risk. Each installation phase will be fenced
off  from  the  operating  floor,  and  the  site  teams
will agree a daily access plan with the supplier so
that forklift routes and fire exits stay clear.
7. Assumptions and Data Sources
Volume forecasts use the commercial plan agreed
in  September  2026,  which  assumes  11  percent
growth  in  2027  and  a  peak  week  2.3  times  the
size  of  an  average  week.  Labour  costs  use  the
2027  pay  award  and  include  employer  on-costs,
overtime  premiums  and  agency  fees  for  tempo-
rary staff.
Equipment   prices   are   taken   from   the   three
supplier  quotations  and  are  held  for  ninety  days.
They  include  delivery,  installation  and  the  first
year  of  software  licences,  but  exclude  building
alterations,  which  are  estimated  separately  by  the
property team.
Benefits  are  counted  only  where  they  can  be
measured  from  existing  data.  Improvements  in
customer  satisfaction,  lower  staff  turnover  and
reduced  injury  rates  are  expected  but  were  not
included in the payback figures.
8. Recommendations
The engineering group recommends approval of a
combined  capital  budget  of  GBP  3,240,000  for
2027,  covering  handheld  scanners  at  all  three
hubs, a cross-belt sorter at Leeds and a robotic put
wall at Glasgow.
The  group  further  recommends  that  the  purchase
order  for  the  Leeds  sorter  be  issued  in  January
2027,  that  the  interface  upgrade  be  completed
before  any  automated  system  goes  live,  and  that
Bristol  be  reviewed  again  in  the  first  quarter  of
2028.
Progress  will  be  reported  to  the  operations  board
every   month,   with   a   full   post-implementation
review   of   throughput,   accuracy   and   cost   six
months after each go-live.
